How I think about this

A contract is never finished. Keeping it alive is the job.

Every business, every agency, every program runs on contracts — and no contract is ever done. Laws get passed. Lawsuits get filed. New situations appear that nobody wrote language for, because you can’t contract for what you don’t yet know. So the agreement keeps evolving, and someone has to negotiate each new iteration, hold the line on the terms that matter, and make sure the operation underneath actually delivers what was promised.

That is the work I’ve done for twenty years: the negotiation, the evolving language, and the operations that turn a signed agreement into something that functions.

The market, honestly

Agencies have contracting staff. So do the payers. So why is this work still posted?

It’s a fair question — and the answer is the whole opportunity. Government agencies and health plans both employ people who do contracting, yet they keep posting solicitations and SOWs for exactly this work. Not because the wheel needs reinventing, but because of five gaps their permanent staff can’t or aren’t allowed to close.

Capacity

Permanent staff are maxed out. CalAIM, Prop 1/BHSA, and new programs spike the workload faster than existing headcount can clear it.

Headcount freezes

Budgets allow contractor spend but not new full-time hires. So the work goes out as a contract or SOW instead of a job posting — same work, different bucket.

Vacancy & interim

A contracting lead leaves. They need six to twelve months of experienced coverage while they recruit a permanent replacement.

Finite projects

A network build, a delegation audit, a procurement cycle — real work with an end date that doesn’t justify a permanent hire.

Specialized expertise

Delegated credentialing, NCQA/URAC readiness, behavioral-health network build — niche work their generalist contracting staff often don’t have.

That gap — the overflow, the vacancies, the specialized and time-boxed pieces — is exactly where an outside contractor or SME earns their fee. It’s where I come in.

Why now

California is rebuilding, and the rebuild is made of contracts.

CalAIM, Proposition 1, and the Behavioral Health Services Act are remaking who delivers care, how they’re contracted, and how programs are run — now, across the state. That is a mountain of agreements to negotiate and operations to stand up, at exactly the moment plans, counties, and agencies have the least slack to do it in-house.

People who can both negotiate the contract and run the operation underneath it are scarce. That is the combination I bring.
